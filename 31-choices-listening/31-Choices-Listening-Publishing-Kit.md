## 3. 500-Word Author Bio (Marketing Team Use)

M.E. Reach was born and raised on the Mississippi Gulf Coast, and listening has been the job in the rooms where he has spent his adult life. He is a decorated U.S. Army veteran. For sixteen years he has worked with teens, young adults, and adults across five states and in two foreign countries. The pages do not name the units, the states, or the countries. What they establish is a life with people under pressure, in more than one culture, long enough that paying attention has become a conviction rather than a tip.

That conviction is the book. *31 Choices: Listening* starts from a plain observation: we think we are better listeners than we are, and the urgent keeps tugging us off the important. The people who pay for that are usually the ones closest to us. Reach does not answer with a communication technique. He opens Proverbs and stays there for thirty-one days. Wisdom, he writes, is already calling aloud in the streets. The failure is not that she is quiet. The failure is that we hear her and do not respond.

The sample days show how he teaches. Day 1, on Proverbs 1:20–22, asks the reader to listen for wisdom in ordinary places: a garden, a comment from someone close, even a silence where a voice for justice ought to be. Day 2, on Proverbs 2:1–2, turns treasure from a childhood fantasy into a command. Treasure his words. Incline your ear. Clear the chest of whatever has crowded him out. Day 3, on Proverbs 15:14, uses hide-and-seek. Seeking God is not wandering until something happens. It is the whole attention. The fool, Reach says, is content to stay foolish, often on the excuse of being too busy. The way out he offers is small on purpose. Pray, and start again today.

The voice stays warm and unbranded. He writes as a father of five who makes up songs with his wife to annoy the children, who drinks root beer, who travels, and who calls his own writing occasional. The book does not present him as a professional author with a platform. It presents a month of choices from a man who has had to listen for a living, and who thinks Proverbs already knew how.

He is writing for people who are not short on noise: parents, people who work with the young, anyone who feels the day tilt from the person in front of them toward the next urgent thing. The book is short on theory. Listen to wisdom. Treasure the words. Seek with everything you have. Then do it again tomorrow.

A legal name, a church, and the details of his service were not in the manuscript. Until he supplies them, the bio that can ship is this one: Gulf Coast, veteran, sixteen years with people across five states and two countries, a wife, five children, and a month of Proverbs on learning to listen.

## 5. 350-Word Book Summary (Amazon / IngramSpark)

**You have two ears and one mouth. Most days, the ratio does not show.**

We think we are good listeners. Then the day fills up, the urgent crowds out the important, and we realize we have not really heard the people closest to us — or God. *31 Choices: Listening* is a month of practice against that habit, drawn from Proverbs and written by M.E. Reach.

It is not a lecture on communication skills. Each day is a choice. A few verses. A plainspoken reflection. One thing to do before the day gets away. Reach's claim is the one Proverbs keeps making: wisdom is not whispering. She calls aloud in the streets, at the gates, in the open square. We hear her. We often refuse to answer. These thirty-one days are for closing that gap, a little at a time, with God and with the people in the room.

The first three days show the path. **Listening to Wisdom** asks you to notice wisdom wherever she is already speaking — a garden, a comment from someone close, even a silence where a word of justice ought to be. **What Do You Treasure?** takes the childhood thrill of buried treasure and turns it around. The command is not to go find a prize. It is to treasure his words, incline your ear, and clear the chest of whatever has crowded him out. **Seek with Everything** remembers hide-and-seek. Finding God is not aimless wandering. It takes the whole attention, including the discomfort of actually looking. The alternative, Proverbs says, is a fool content to feed on foolishness, often because he is "too busy."

Reach writes as a Mississippi Gulf Coast native, a decorated U.S. Army veteran, and a man who has spent sixteen years with teens, young adults, and adults across five states and two countries. He is also a husband and a father of five, which is its own school of listening. The tone is warm, specific, and short enough to finish before the kettle boils.

One month. One choice a day. Listen twice as much as you speak.

*(348 words)*

## 21. Reader Angles (Content Hooks)

1. **"Two ears, one mouth."** The epigraph is the ad. It is not a Bible verse, so do not caption it as one. It is still the line people will repeat.
2. **Thirty-one days, not a year.** A month is a promise a busy reader can keep. Say the length in every ad.
3. **Wisdom is already shouting.** Proverbs 1 is the opposite of "listen for a still small voice," and that contrast is a useful post.
4. **Treasure, don't just hunt.** Day 2 flips the childhood treasure story. Good for a short video.
5. **Hide-and-seek as discipleship.** Day 3 is concrete enough to tell in under a minute.
6. **"Too busy" is the fool's excuse.** Sharp, and fair, because he offers the exit in the same paragraph.
7. **A father of five who writes songs to annoy them.** The bio is more memorable than a list of credentials. Use it.
8. **A series people can join.** If a second *31 Choices* volume is real, say so. If it is only a hope, sell this book as a complete month and mention the series lightly.
